Giving someone freedom over *how* to do the work is different from giving them the right to decide *what* the work should achieve. When a leader tells an experienced person to "just figure it out," they often believe they're granting autonomy. If the strategy underneath is unresolved (conflicting goals, unclear priorities, no agreement on what can be sacrificed), they're actually handing over a strategic decision without the authority to make it.

## Why experienced people ask more, not less

Experience reduces the need for *instructions* but increases sensitivity to *strategic ambiguity*. A junior person tends to accept an assignment as given and work out how to satisfy it. An experienced person notices when satisfying the literal request would undermine the outcome it was meant to serve, and also notices the incentives, constraints and second-order effects that should change the approach.

Uncertainty and conflict are different problems here. Experts deal well with uncertainty: they form hypotheses, experiment and learn their way forward. Expertise can't resolve a genuine conflict between organisational objectives, though. Only someone with the authority to trade one goal off against another can do that. Asking for that call is asking for clarity, not certainty.

So the experienced person who keeps coming back with questions isn't being "high maintenance". They're pointing at a decision that belongs to someone else.

## Connection to ownership

In the [Ownership Trio](/notes/ownership-trio/), this is a missing **mandate**. The experienced person has the knowledge and will carry the responsibility for the outcome, but the authority to settle the strategic trade-off sits elsewhere. Left unaddressed, it turns into the "Foot Soldier" pattern among the [Broken Ownership Archetypes](/notes/broken-ownership-archetypes/), with the frustration and attrition that come with it.

The fix is either to resolve the strategy (outcome, priorities, constraints, acceptable sacrifices) or to explicitly delegate the authority to decide it. That's the job of a good [Engineering Strategy](/notes/engineering-strategy/): it does the hard prioritisation up front so people executing don't have to guess.

## The quiet failure mode

Experienced people can tell when the organisation has run out of patience for their questions. Told to stop overthinking, they may appear to fall in line, but often they're routing around the unresolved issue: avoiding irreversible commitments, keeping options open and waiting for the flawed logic to show itself. What looks like new alignment may be skilled hedging. Sometimes a [right no is better than a wrong yes](/notes/right-no-vs-wrong-yes/), and pushing someone into a yes doesn't make the underlying conflict go away.
