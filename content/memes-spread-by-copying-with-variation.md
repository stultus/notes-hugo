Richard Dawkins coined *meme* in *The Selfish Gene* (1976) for a unit of culture, such as a tune, a catchphrase, a fashion or a way of building an arch, that spreads from person to person by imitation. He chose the word to echo *gene*. Like genes, memes are copied, the copies vary a little, and the variants that suit people best spread while the rest die out. Nobody designs the outcome; selection does.

Words are a clean example. Needing one more sentence for a Guardian article in 2004, Ben Hammersley made up several names for downloadable audio: audioblogging, podcasting, GuerillaMedia. Only "podcasting" got copied, and now it's in dictionaries ([Podcast Etymology](/notes/podcast-etymology/)). First variation, then selection.

Internet memes make the process visible in real time. An image template is copied, recaptioned and remixed thousands of times, usually without anyone asking permission or knowing who made the first version. The value is in the remixing, not the original, which makes memes the plainest everyday evidence that [all creative work is derivative](/notes/all-creative-work-is-derivative/). Most reuse someone else's photograph or film still. Whether that's legal depends on local exceptions (fair use in the US, fair dealing in India), but in practice memes spread faster than anyone could enforce against them.

Nina Paley called her short films about copying *Minute Memes*. They were made to be copied, and licensed so that anyone may. An older example in this garden is the [Linux parody of Tolkien's Ring Verse](/notes/sources/linux-ring-verse-parody/), a mutated copy of a famous poem that spread by being pasted into email signatures.
