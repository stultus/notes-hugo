When copyright expires, a work enters the public domain, and anyone may copy, adapt and remix it without asking or paying. That makes the public domain the raw material of new work. If [all creative work is derivative](/notes/all-creative-work-is-derivative/), what creators can legally build on depends on what's in it.

Nina Paley's *All Creative Work Is Derivative* (2010) is an example. She animated it from photographs of sculptures at the Met, and because those sculptures are centuries old she needed nobody's permission. The same film made from twentieth-century sculpture would need clearances from many rights holders, and probably wouldn't exist.

Paley knew the opposite case first-hand. Her feature film *Sita Sings the Blues* (2008) used 1920s recordings of songs sung by Annette Hanshaw. The recordings were out of copyright but the songs themselves weren't, and licensing fees held up the film's release. Afterwards she released the film under a Creative Commons licence, adding it to the commons by choice.

Expiry has visible effects. A. A. Milne's *Winnie-the-Pooh* (1926) entered the US public domain in 2022, and within a year an independent horror film, *Winnie-the-Pooh: Blood and Honey*, had been made from it.

The law sets how long the wait is. In India, copyright generally lasts the author's lifetime plus 60 years; in the US, lifetime plus 70. Each extension keeps works out of the commons for longer and shrinks what later creators can build on. Ideas are free from the start, because [copyright protects expression, not ideas](/notes/copyright-protects-expression-not-ideas/). The public domain is where the expression itself becomes free.
